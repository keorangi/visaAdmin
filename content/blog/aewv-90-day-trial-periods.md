An employment agreement with a 90-day trial clause is standard issue for most New Zealand employers — and a live compliance defect in an AEWV hire. From 29 October 2023, accredited employers cannot use trial periods when hiring on the Accredited Employer Work Visa; a Job Check application with a trial period in the agreement can be declined, and using one puts accreditation itself at risk.[^1] Eight weeks after that ban landed, the law moved the other way for everyone else: from 23 December 2023, 90-day trial periods became available to all employers, of any size.[^2] The two rules are still in force, still pointing in opposite directions — and the single-template habit is where they collide.

## Why this matters now

The divergence is old enough that it has stopped being news and become a trap. Employment New Zealand's current guidance — republished in March 2026 — carries the rule in one line: "Immigration New Zealand does not allow trial periods for migrant workers employed on an Accredited Employer Work Visa".[^3] And INZ's current Job Check employer checklist lists a trial period among the clauses an employment agreement cannot include, alongside unlawful deductions and unacceptable bonding clauses.[^4] An employer who last reviewed their template when trial periods "came back" in December 2023 may be carrying the defect into every Job Check since.

## What changed in October 2023 for AEWV hires

INZ announced the restriction on 18 October 2023, effective 29 October 2023, with the stated aim of encouraging accredited employers "to treat migrant fairly, and only recruit someone when they have a genuine labour need or skills gap to fill".[^1] The mechanics are blunt: employment agreements provided with Job Check applications must not include a trial period, and an application under assessment that includes one will be declined or met with a request for updated information — regardless of when it was submitted.[^1] Beyond the Job Check, INZ was explicit about the accreditation exposure: employers who use a trial period in employment agreements "risk having their accreditation revoked", with breaches detectable up front at the Job Check or later through post-decision and reaccreditation checks.[^1]

## What changed in December 2023 for everyone else

Until late 2023, 90-day trial periods were limited to employers with fewer than 20 employees. The Employment Relations (Trial Periods) Amendment Act 2023 removed the size limit, and from 23 December 2023 any employer can agree a trial period with a new employee.[^2] INZ addressed the apparent contradiction within days of the announcement: accredited employers "are not captured in this legislation so this will not apply to accredited employers and those on the AEWV".[^5]

That framing matters for how you read the rule. The AEWV prohibition is not employment law — it lives in the immigration instructions that govern accreditation and Job Checks. The Employment Relations Act says you may use a trial period; INZ says a Job Check with one in the agreement fails. Both are true at once.

## Where the line actually sits

The prohibition attaches to the AEWV channel, not to migrant workers as a class. A worker on an open work visa — a partner's visa, a Post Study Work Visa, a working holiday visa — sits under the general Employment Relations Act rules, where a trial period is lawful if it is done correctly.[^3] Done correctly is its own list: the trial must be agreed before work starts, be recorded in the employment agreement, run no longer than 90 calendar days, and apply only to someone who has not worked for the employer before — and if the employee has not signed the agreement before starting work, the trial period is not valid.[^3]

That "not worked for the employer before" condition quietly answers a common follow-up: an existing AEWV worker moving to a new agreement — a promotion, a residence-pathway transition — could never validly be put on a trial period anyway, whatever their visa.[^3]

Two boundary cases are worth naming. A candidate on an open visa hired on a trial period who later needs an AEWV — the agreement backing the Job Check cannot carry the trial clause forward. And a mixed workforce on one template — every AEWV hire signed on the standard agreement is a potential defect sitting in the personnel file, waiting for a [post-accreditation check]({{< ref "/blog/post-accreditation-check-what-to-expect" >}}) to find it.

## The template trap

The failure mode here is administrative, not strategic. Nobody decides to breach; someone reuses the standard template. The fix is equally administrative: an AEWV-specific agreement template with no trial clause, and a check in the Job Check preparation that the right template was used. Declined Job Checks cost weeks — the [declined-reasons patterns]({{< ref "/blog/aewv-job-check-declined-reasons" >}}) are mostly evidence problems, and this one is among the cheapest to prevent. The downstream costs of the accreditation-level version — revocation, the published list — are covered in the [cost of getting it wrong article]({{< ref "/blog/cost-of-aewv-compliance-failure" >}}).

## What to do

1. Search your current employment agreement templates for trial-period clauses, and split out an AEWV version without one — today, not at the next Job Check.[^4]
2. Audit the agreements of existing AEWV workers hired since 29 October 2023. If any contain a trial clause, take advice on remediation before INZ finds it at a check.[^1]
3. Add a template check to your Job Check preparation list: the agreement backing the application cannot include a trial period, unlawful deductions, or unacceptable bonding clauses.[^4]
4. For non-AEWV hires where you do use trials, follow the validity checklist — agreed before start, in the signed agreement, 90 days maximum, first employment with you — or the clause fails as employment law before immigration law ever looks at it.[^3]
5. Brief whoever issues offer letters. The person who picks the template is the control point.

## What we don't know

Whether INZ treats probationary periods — which Employment New Zealand distinguishes from trial periods, and which can run for any length of time — as acceptable in AEWV employment agreements is not stated in the public guidance we reviewed. INZ's own checklist prohibits "a trial period" and links out to Employment New Zealand's page on the distinction, but does not say where probationary periods stand; treat that as a question for your adviser, not an assumption. INZ also publishes no figures on how many Job Checks are declined for trial-period clauses specifically, so we cannot say how often this trap actually springs — only that the rule is current and the detection paths are named.

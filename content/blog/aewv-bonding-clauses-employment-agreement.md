A clause requiring an employee to repay training or relocation costs if they leave within a year is unremarkable in a New Zealand employment agreement. Put the same clause in an AEWV agreement and three things happen. It is named on INZ's Job Check checklist as an unacceptable bonding clause.[^1] It runs into an instruction that bars passing recruitment, training or equipment costs to the worker at all.[^2] And where the repayment comes out of wages, Employment New Zealand's guidance indicates it may be treated as a premium — which is unlawful under the Wages Protection Act 1983.[^5] [^6]

## Why this matters now

The employment agreement is assessed at Job Check, before anyone has been hired, on the strength of a declaration you make.[^3] Nothing re-checks it at the visa stage unless an officer decides to look. That means a non-compliant clause can sit unnoticed through a Job Check approval, a visa grant, and two years of employment — and surface for the first time in a post-accreditation check, by which point it has been in force the whole time.

## The Job Check declaration is narrower than most contracts

Employers applying for a Job Check declare that the employment agreement for the proposed job will include all mandatory clauses required by employment law, include details about paid leave entitlements, include the maximum hours before overtime rates apply and the overtime rate, include the maximum hours the employee may be asked to work, include the pay period, and "only include clauses that comply with employment law."[^3] It must "not include a trial provision as defined in section 67A(2) of the Employment Relations Act 2000."[^3]

INZ's employer checklist puts the same ground in plainer words. The agreement cannot contain a trial period, "clauses that do not comply with employment law", "unlawful deductions", or "unacceptable bonding clauses — these are provisions in an employment agreement that require an employee to repay money to the employer if the employee leaves the employment within a certain timeframe."[^1]

That definition is broad. It does not distinguish between repaying a training course, a relocation package, or a visa fee. The test is the shape of the clause: repay money, if you leave, within a period.

## The cost list is specific, and it includes your own application fees

Separately from the bonding point, accreditation instructions bar passing costs on at all. An employer "must not pass on recruitment, training or equipment costs, in New Zealand and outside of New Zealand, to Accredited Employer work visa holders or applicants."[^2] The list that follows is not exhaustive but is unusually concrete:

- advertising costs
- recruitment agency fees
- employer accreditation and Job Check application fees, "and any other associated costs such as immigration adviser fees"
- compulsory training and induction costs related to the job, including on-the-job training
- health and safety equipment required to undertake employment safely
- branded uniforms
- trade testing, including testing centre, tester accommodation and salary costs
- tools where the ownership of the tools is retained by the employer[^2]

The same bar is carried into the acceptable-employment instructions that govern the job itself: the employment "must not include the employer passing any recruitment, compulsory training or equipment costs or fees to the employee."[^4] The [accreditation fees note]({{< ref "/blog/aewv-accreditation-fees-what-they-cover" >}}) covers what those application fees are and what they buy.

Read the third bullet twice. Your accreditation fee, your Job Check fee, and your immigration adviser's invoice are all costs you cannot recover from the worker — not directly, and not through a repayment clause later.

## A bond taken from wages may be a premium

Employment law reaches the same conduct from a different direction, and with sharper teeth.

Section 12A of the Wages Protection Act 1983 states that "no employer or person engaged on behalf of the employer shall seek or receive any premium in respect of the employment of any person, whether the premium is sought or received from the person employed or proposed to be employed or from any other person."[^6] Money received in breach is recoverable from the employer as a debt, and proceedings can be brought in the Employment Relations Authority by the worker or by a Labour Inspector on their behalf.[^6] A penalty may also be imposed by the Authority.[^6]

Employment New Zealand's guidance on deductions puts it directly: "It is illegal for employers to ask employees for money, sometimes known as a premium or fee for giving them a job or keeping them in a job." It then extends the point to bonds: "Some deduction or bonding arrangements where employees have agreed to have amounts deducted from their wages for on-the-job training or recruitment costs if they end their employment within a certain time may also be considered premiums."[^5]

"May also be considered" is the operative hedge, and we are not going to over-read it. But an accredited employer facing that assessment is facing it under two regimes at once — an employment-standards question and an accreditation requirement that has no hedge in it.

Immigration instructions add a third: INZ "will decline an application for a work visa where it considers that the employment was offered as a result of payment made or promised by the applicant (or their agent) to the employer (or their agent) in exchange for securing that offer of employment."[^7]

{{< callout type="info" >}}
**Our take.** The commercial instinct behind a bond is legitimate. You have spent real money bringing someone to New Zealand, and you would like some protection against them leaving in month three. The rules do not accommodate that instinct, and the reason is visible in their construction — a debt owed to an employer by a worker whose visa is tied to that employer changes the balance of the relationship. If retention is the problem you are solving, the levers left open are pay, progression, and the residence pathway. Repayment clauses are not among them.
{{< /callout >}}

## Adding a clause after the Job Check does not fix it

A common workaround is to keep the Job Check agreement clean and introduce the repayment provision later — at the visa stage, at onboarding, or in a side letter covering relocation.

Instructions close that route. All terms and conditions of the employment offered "must be the same as, or more favourable to the applicant than, those declared or provided (if applicable) as part of the Job Check application."[^7] A repayment obligation that was not in the declared agreement is not more favourable. The same instruction requires the employment to continue to meet the acceptable-employment requirements in force when the visa application is made.[^7]

Post-hire is no safer. The cost-passing bar in accreditation instructions applies to AEWV "holders or applicants" — a holder is someone already working for you.[^2]

## What to do

1. Search every AEWV employment agreement and offer letter for the words "repay", "reimburse", "clawback", "bond", and "deduction". Read what each one does, not what it is called.
2. Check relocation, immigration-fee, and training-cost arrangements that sit outside the agreement — side letters, policies, and email undertakings count as arrangements even when they are not clauses.
3. Reconcile your recruitment cost ledger against the WA2.10.10 list. Any line recovered from a worker, in any form, is the problem to fix first.[^2]
4. Confirm no AEWV agreement contains a trial provision. The [trial periods note]({{< ref "/blog/aewv-90-day-trial-periods" >}}) covers why the AEWV rule and the general employment rule diverged in late 2023.
5. Where you find a clause, take advice before amending. Removing it changes the agreement, and the version INZ approved at Job Check is the reference point.
6. Add the check to your Job Check pack, not just your contract template. The declaration is made per application.[^3]

## What we don't know

INZ publishes the definition of an unacceptable bonding clause but not the boundary. A genuine, freely agreed loan for a purpose unrelated to the job — a car, a rental bond — is not obviously "recruitment, training or equipment costs", but we have not found published guidance stating where the line falls, and the checklist definition catches any provision to repay money on early exit.[^1]

We also do not know how often bonding clauses feature in declined Job Checks or in post-accreditation check findings. INZ names them as a checklist item but does not publish outcome data at that level, so the practical enforcement rate is unknown to us.

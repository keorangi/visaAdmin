Checking employee work rights stopped being one legal question and became two on 6 January 2024. The Worker Protection (Migrant and Other Employees) Act 2023 rewrote the employer offence in the Immigration Act: the criminal offence now requires knowledge, while a new infringement tier imposes fixed fees with no knowledge element at all.[^1] A lot of guidance still circulating online describes the old provisions — including a "reasonable precautions and due diligence" defence that was repealed the same day.[^1]

## Why this matters now

The infringement tier is not theoretical: INZ operates an immigration employment infringement scheme with $1,000 fees for individual employers and $3,000 for body corporates, per employee, with stand-down periods that put the employer's name on a published list.[^2] The [cost of getting it wrong article]({{< ref "/blog/cost-of-aewv-compliance-failure" >}}) covers the consequences end to end. This article is about the checking obligation that sits in front of them — as the statute actually reads in 2026.

## The criminal offence now turns on knowledge

Section 350(1) of the Immigration Act 2009, as replaced in January 2024, reads: an employer commits an offence who "allows, or continues to allow, any person to work in that employer's service, knowing that the person is not entitled under this Act to do that work".[^1] Conviction carries a fine of up to $50,000.[^3]

Knowledge is the hinge — and the Act closes the obvious escape. Under section 350(6), an employer is *treated* as knowing a person is not entitled to work if an immigration officer has told them so in writing at any time in the preceding 12 months.[^1] Once that letter arrives, "we didn't realise" is no longer available for a year. One carve-out runs the other way: section 350(7) protects an employer who keeps a person on only to meet the minimum notice requirements of their employment agreement on termination.[^1]

Above section 350 sits section 351 — exploitation of unlawful employees and temporary workers — where serious breaches of the Holidays Act, Minimum Wage Act, or Wages Protection Act against such workers, or acts preventing them leaving or seeking their entitlements, carry up to 7 years' imprisonment or a $100,000 fine.[^3] The [Worker Protection Act article]({{< ref "/blog/aewv-worker-protection-act-employer-obligations" >}}) covers that layer.

## The infringement tier does not

The same 2024 amendment inserted section 359A, and it reads differently on purpose. An employer "must not" allow a person not entitled to work to do that work, or employ a person "in a manner that is inconsistent with a work-related condition of that person's visa".[^1] There is no knowledge element. Contravention is an infringement offence: $1,000 per employee for an individual employer, $3,000 per employee for a body corporate, with a court able to impose up to double the total fees instead.[^1] A third limb makes failing to produce documents requested by an immigration officer under section 275A within the required time its own infringement, at $1,000.[^1]

This is where the practical exposure sits for an employer who never intended anything. A visa that lapsed unnoticed, a worker rostered outside their visa conditions — the [different-site pattern]({{< ref "/blog/different-site-infringement-aewv" >}}) is the most cited example — none of it requires INZ to show the employer knew. Infringements also carry stand-downs from hiring migrant workers: 6 months for a single notice, up to 12 for multiple, and up to 24 months where a court fine reaches $25,000 or more.[^2]

## The two ways to check

INZ names two checking routes.[^4] The first is VisaView, INZ's free online service for registered employers: an enquiry confirms whether a prospective or current employee holds a valid visa, the conditions on it, and its expiry date, and a separate disclosure process can confirm New Zealand passport information for citizen candidates.[^5] Enquiries are saved in the employer's account, and INZ's own guidance states these records "can be used as evidence of your compliance with the Immigration Act 2009".[^4]

The second is a document check: a passport with a valid New Zealand work or residence visa, an Australian passport, or a refugee travel document with a valid visa — with INZ suggesting employers keep a copy.[^4]

VisaView's limits are stated plainly by INZ and worth taking seriously. It shows only the current visa — not applications INZ has not yet decided, and not travel movements.[^5] And it does not confirm identity: the enquiry checks visa status against the details entered, so the employer still needs to sight original photo identification.[^5]

## What checking buys you now

Here is the shift worth absorbing. Under the repealed provisions, checking fed a statutory defence — the old wording made lack of knowledge a defence only where the employer had taken "reasonable precautions and exercised due diligence". Those subsections are gone.[^1] Checking now does something more direct: it stops the breach existing at all. Against a no-fault infringement, the only reliable protection is that the person was, in fact, entitled to do the work — which is precisely what a day-one VisaView enquiry and an expiry-tracking habit are for. The saved enquiry then documents that the employer's systems work, which is what INZ looks at when accreditation and [post-accreditation checks]({{< ref "/blog/post-accreditation-check-what-to-expect" >}}) put record-keeping under the light.

## What to do

1. Register for VisaView if you have not, and make an enquiry part of the pre-start checklist for every hire — not only the obviously-migrant ones. An Australian passport or NZ citizenship claim is checkable too.[^4]
2. Sight original photo ID alongside the VisaView enquiry; the tool does not confirm identity.[^5]
3. Re-check at visa expiry, not just at hire. Section 359A attaches to "allows, or continues to allow" — the ongoing state, not the hiring moment.[^1]
4. If an immigration officer writes to you about a worker's entitlement, act the same day and file the letter with its date: it starts a 12-month period of deemed knowledge under section 350(6).[^1]
5. Keep visa conditions — employer, role, location, hours — next to the roster, so a condition mismatch is visible before it becomes a section 359A(1)(b) breach.[^1]

## What we don't know

How INZ chooses between an infringement notice and prosecution where conduct could support either is not published in the material we reviewed. Nor is there public guidance on how a genuine, documented checking system weighs in that discretion — INZ says VisaView records evidence compliance, but not what forbearance, if any, that evidence earns once a breach has occurred. And the interaction between an Interim Visa and a VisaView enquiry on the day a visa expires has practical edges we have flagged separately in the [interim visa article]({{< ref "/blog/aewv-interim-visa-work-rights" >}}).

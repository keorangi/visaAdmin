If you renew your AEWV employer accreditation early, you do not get the unused time back. Immigration New Zealand renews accreditation from the date it approves the application, not from the date your old accreditation was due to expire.[^1] Apply three months out and you donate most of those three months. Apply after expiry and you lose access to interim accreditation. The window that works is narrower than it looks, and it is the part of renewal least often described.

## Why this matters now

There are almost 30,000 accredited employers in New Zealand, as at 30 June 2026.[^2] Accreditation granted for the first time runs 12 months; renewals for standard and high-volume employers run 24 months.[^1] That means a large share of the employer base is now on its second, third, or fourth cycle, and renewal has become routine administration rather than a first-time event. INZ's only prompt is an email two weeks before the expiry date.[^1] Two weeks is enough time to notice. It is not enough time to assemble a renewal application, and it is well inside the five weeks INZ itself recommends you allow.[^1]

## Renewal runs from the approval date, not your expiry date

This is the rule that should set your timing, and it is easy to miss because it sits inside a worked example rather than in a heading.

INZ states it plainly: "If we approve your application, we renew your accreditation from the date we approve it."[^1] The example on the same page uses an accreditation due to expire on 30 August, a renewal submitted on 1 June, and an approval on 1 July. The new accreditation begins 1 July.[^1]

Read that against a 24-month renewal. Approval on 1 July gives you an accreditation running to 1 July two years later — not 30 August two years later. The two months between approval and the old expiry date are gone. They are not added on, and there is no mechanism described anywhere in INZ's published guidance for reclaiming them.

The practical consequence: every week you apply earlier than you need to is a week removed from the far end of the next cycle. Over several renewals that compounds into an extra application and an extra fee.

## Interim accreditation is the reason not to leave it too late

The counterweight is interim accreditation, and it has a hard cut-off.

If your accreditation expires while INZ is still deciding your renewal, you may be granted an interim accreditation lasting three months, or until the application is decided, whichever comes first.[^1] [^3] It starts when your current accreditation expires.[^3] With it, you can apply for a new Job Check and use an existing job token to ask a migrant to apply for an AEWV.[^1]

Three conditions attach. INZ's public guidance says you must have applied before your current accreditation expired, that the application is still in progress, and that you applied for the right accreditation type for your business.[^1] The Operational Manual is stricter on the first of these: it requires an application that "has been accepted for processing before their current accreditation has expired."[^3] Submitted and accepted are not the same event, and the gap between them is not something you control.

There is also a cap. Where the renewal is for standard accreditation, interim accreditation is granted only if the employer does not have more than five jobs associated with them.[^3]

{{< callout type="info" >}}
**Our take.** The two rules pull against each other, and that is the whole of the timing problem. Applying early costs you accreditation time you have paid for. Applying late risks a gap in which you cannot issue a Job Check. Somewhere between four and eight weeks out is where both risks are small — early enough to be accepted for processing and decided before expiry, late enough that you are not writing off months. That is a judgement, not a rule, and INZ does not publish a recommended lead time beyond "allow at least 5 weeks."
{{< /callout >}}

## How long the renewed accreditation lasts

Three durations, set out in the Operational Manual and reflected on the public page:[^1] [^3]

- **24 months** — where you have held accreditation before, it has not lapsed for 12 months or more, and you are not being granted triangular accreditation.
- **12 months** — first-time accreditation, or triangular employer accreditation, or where the previous accreditation lapsed 12 months or more ago.
- **12 months** — for any accreditation renewed more than 12 months after it expired, regardless of type.[^1]

One quieter rule sits alongside these. If you upgrade from standard to high-volume accreditation part-way through a period, "the expiry date of the initial accreditation remains the same."[^3] Upgrading does not restart the clock. You pay the NZD $505 upgrade fee and keep your existing expiry date.[^4]

Accreditation "expires at the beginning of the day after the date specified as the expiry date."[^3] The expiry date itself is a day you are still accredited.

## Renewal is a look-back over the period you just finished

The most common misreading of renewal is that it repeats the original application. It does not. It examines what you did with the accreditation you already held.

An immigration officer assessing a subsequent application "must assess whether the employer has complied with" a defined set of requirements "since their previous accreditation was granted."[^5] That set covers settlement support activities, specific immigration standards, business standards, the triangular-placement requirements where they apply, reasonable steps on qualification and skill, and the duty to tell INZ about changes in circumstances.[^5]

If you have not complied, the instruction is not discretionary at the first step: the application "must be declined" unless you have rectified the non-compliance promptly, taken sufficient steps to address its cause, and satisfied INZ you will comply in future.[^5] The manual then adds the sentence that decides how you should prepare: "The onus is on the employer to satisfy INZ that they meet (b) above."[^5]

One item deserves separate mention because it can decline a renewal on its own. Employers must tell INZ within 10 working days of changes to key persons or business structure, and when an AEWV or Peak Seasonal Visa holder's employment ends more than a month before their visa expires.[^6] The manual states that "an employer accreditation renewal application may be declined if an employer has failed to advise INZ of any changes outlined in (a) or (b) above within the required timeframe."[^6] A missed notification from 14 months ago is live at renewal.

Post-accreditation checks are a separate process — INZ says so directly: "Post-accreditation checks are separate to getting re-accreditation."[^2] Passing one does not pre-clear the other.

## What the application itself involves

The mechanics are short. You log into Immigration Online with RealMe, open the organisation under "My accredited organisations," choose "Renew accreditation," complete the form and pay.[^1] Fees are NZD $775 for standard, $1,280 for high-volume and $4,060 for triangular employer accreditation.[^4]

Two process details are worth knowing before you start. If INZ asks for further information, it sends a link allowing up to five additional documents, with a 10 MB limit per file.[^1] And you can change accreditation type at renewal — from high-volume to standard, for example — without a separate application.[^1]

On timing, INZ recommends allowing at least five weeks.[^1] Its published wait times tell a different story: accreditation renewals processed in the four weeks to the date of access showed an average wait of four days, with most completed within 1.5 weeks.[^7] That page is updated weekly and the figures move, so treat the five-week recommendation as your planning assumption and the wait-times page as a check on the day.

## What to do

1. Find your accreditation expiry date in Immigration Online under "My accredited organisations," and diary it now with a lead alert at 10 weeks — not at INZ's two-week reminder.
2. Decide your submission target. If you have no reason to move early, four to eight weeks out balances the approval-date cost against the risk of needing interim accreditation.
3. Before you submit, list every key-person change, business-structure change, and AEWV worker departure since your last accreditation was granted, and confirm each was notified to INZ within 10 working days. Note any that were not — you will need the rectification story, not a gap.
4. Pull your settlement support records for every AEWV worker recruited since the last grant, and check each was given the information within one month of starting.
5. Check your accreditation type still fits. Standard covers hiring up to five migrants; high-volume covers six or more.[^4] If you have outgrown standard, renewing into the wrong type means applying again and paying a second fee, with no refund of the first.[^1]
6. If you are renewing standard accreditation and want interim cover as a fallback, check how many jobs are currently associated with your organisation. More than five and the interim grant is not available.[^3]

## What we don't know

INZ does not publish a recommended lead time beyond "at least 5 weeks," and does not say how long acceptance for processing typically takes after submission. Because interim accreditation depends on acceptance rather than submission,[^3] that gap is a real planning uncertainty and we have not found a published figure for it.

Nor does INZ publish how often interim accreditation is granted, refused, or how often renewals are decided after the old accreditation has expired. The wait-time figures suggest most renewals are decided quickly, but averages do not tell you the shape of the tail — and the tail is where interim accreditation matters.
